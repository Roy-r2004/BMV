# Top consultancies sell traceable judgment, not slides

A top-tier strategy or operations engagement ends with a three-tier package, not a report: a short decision document built to be approved in one meeting (a 35-slide deck body or a 6-page executive summary), an in-document appendix whose hinge is a recommendation register giving every recommendation an impact, cost, timeframe and owner, and a separate compendium of deep-dives, white paper and working materials that can run to ten times the length and is often withheld or flagged "not all verified". BCG's 57-slide Dallas council deck sat on a 94-page white paper, a 115-page deep-dive deck and 137 pages of working documents ([Slideworks](https://slideworks.io/resources/what-real-consulting-deliverables-look-like-bcg-example)); McKinsey's Lebanon Economic Vision put a ~150-slide executive summary in front of a ~1,000-slide full study ([Lebanon Economic Vision](https://www.economy.gov.lb/media/11893/20181022-1228full-report-en.pdf)). What makes the package worth its fee is not format but traceability: every page carries a one-sentence action title that an exhibit must prove, a source line that names each dataset and ends with "McKinsey analysis" to flag the firm's own computation, numbered footnotes that start "Assumes", and a status sticker ("ESTIMATE", "PRELIMINARY", "ILLUSTRATIVE") that tells the reader how far to trust it. Uncertainty is carried as scenario ranges with the flex rule written in a footnote, never as confidence intervals or red-amber-green ratings. The handover is the design of a management system (register, stage gates, weekly cadence, finance-owned baseline) rather than a Gantt chart, because the firms' own data show first estimates lose ~70% of their value before they reach cash flows ([McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/rts/our-insights/keeping-transformations-on-target)). Clients' verdict is mixed even on the incumbents: only half say value exceeded fees and just 35% for US management consultancies ([Source Global Research](https://www.sourceglobalresearch.com/?p=138877)), and the 2025–26 Deloitte and PwC fabricated-citation scandals mean the first thing a client now checks is whether the footnotes exist. The BCG/Harvard experiment set the exact bar an AI-run consultancy must clear: GPT-4 made consultants' write-ups read better while cutting correctness from 84% to 60–70% on the one task that required triangulating a client's spreadsheet with interviews ([One Useful Thing](https://www.oneusefulthing.org/p/centaurs-and-cyborgs-on-the-jagged)). Beating ChatGPT therefore means proving the triangulation, not polishing the prose.

## The deliverable is three tiers, and the decision document is the smallest

Across every real deliverable that could be read page by page, the order of the decision document is invariant: cover, contents, executive summary (answer first), mandate and method, findings, recommendations, implementation and next steps, appendices. Length varies by two orders of magnitude with format. McKinsey's **Independent Pricing Review for Australia's National Disability Insurance Agency (February 2018) is 103 pages**, of which the executive summary takes pages 3–8, the introduction four pages, consultation input seven, findings thirteen, and the recommendations chapter **59 pages (33–91)**, followed by ten pages of appendices and a glossary ([McKinsey IPR Final Report](https://www.ndis.gov.au/media/359/download)). The recommendations chapter opens by restating the test the whole report answers ("the fundamental test of whether price caps are adequate is whether participants can access quality supports"), says "the IPR team has made 25 recommendations", quantifies the aggregate effect ("improving overall industry margins by 2% to 4%"), and maps each section to numbered recommendations ("Section 4.3 ... covering Recommendations 6 to 9"), with each topic then laid out as heading, an "Issues and evidence" block and the recommendation itself (pp. 34, 48) ([McKinsey IPR Final Report](https://www.ndis.gov.au/media/359/download)).

The deck form inverts the page budget. **BCG's "Loose dogs in Dallas: Strategic Recommendations to Improve Public Safety and Animal Welfare", presented to City Council on 30 August 2016, is 57 slides: 35 in the main body and 22 in the appendix** ([BCG Dallas deck](https://byu-strategy.github.io/management-consulting/resources/reference-decks/bcg-loose-dogs-dallas.pdf)). Slide 2 is an agenda of four words (Context / Key Findings / Recommendations / Next steps) that repeats as a divider before each section; slide 3 states the mandate; slide 4 the evidence base ("100+ stakeholder interviews, ~40 benchmark-city interviews"); slides 9–23 are fifteen finding slides; slide 25 states the answer ("BCG recommends seven actions for Dallas / High level recommendations must be taken as a whole--cherry picking will not work"); slide 26 expands to "28 specific initiatives"; slide 27 sorts them into quick wins, medium-term initiatives and strategic priorities; slide 29 prices them at "$20.9MM" in a waterfall; slide 33 gives next steps; slide 34 names the other three deliverables; and slides 37–43 are one appendix page per recommendation with Initiatives and From/To columns ([BCG Dallas deck](https://byu-strategy.github.io/management-consulting/resources/reference-decks/bcg-loose-dogs-dallas.pdf)). Findings outnumber recommendation slides fifteen to six because the recommendation detail is pushed down a tier, to the appendix one-pagers and to the separate deep-dive deck, which "Opens with an overview of all recommendations, their prioritization, and the total estimated funding need" and then gives each initiative "overview, background and context, key assumptions, sizing of potential, and cost to execute" ([Slideworks](https://slideworks.io/resources/what-real-consulting-deliverables-look-like-bcg-example)). The working documents at the bottom tier "underpin every number" but are "working material and is not all verified" ([Slideworks](https://slideworks.io/resources/what-real-consulting-deliverables-look-like-bcg-example)).

At national scale the same two-tier split simply grows. McKinsey's **Lebanon Economic Vision (2018) is a single 1,274-slide PDF** whose slide 6 brackets the outline as "Executive Summary (~150 pages)" and "Full study (~1000 pages)"; the executive summary is itself a storylined deck with its own contents page, a tracker repeated with the active section highlighted (slides 9, 144), and a corner tag on each slide ("VICIOUS ECONOMIC CYCLE", "FOCUSED APPROACH"), and it ends with the implementation mechanism and next steps before the full study begins at slide 150 ([Lebanon Economic Vision](https://www.economy.gov.lb/media/11893/20181022-1228full-report-en.pdf)). The method slide quantifies the fieldwork the way every public-sector deliverable does: "Interviewed 200+ stakeholders over a period of 8 months", "over 100 documents were reviewed", "A team of ~10 consultants ... with a further 20 global experts" (slide 152) ([Lebanon Economic Vision](https://www.economy.gov.lb/media/11893/20181022-1228full-report-en.pdf)).

The hinge between the decision document and everything below it is the **recommendation register**. In the NDIA report it is Appendix B, "EXHIBIT 9 Summary of recommendations (1/3)", a three-page table whose columns are the recommendation grouped by Terms-of-Reference theme, its "Direct impact on NDIA aspirations" on three dimensions scored with a four-level legend ("Positive impact; Neutral/No impact; Potential minor negative impact e.g. <$50m; Potential significant negative impact e.g. >$50m"), an implementation timeframe (Short <1 year; Medium 1–2 years; Long 3+ years) and the dollar value used to compute the total, e.g. recommendation 3 "$26-75m" ([McKinsey IPR Final Report](https://www.ndis.gov.au/media/359/download)). In the Dallas deck it is the four "Deep-dive on strategic recommendations" tables with columns Priority area | Recommendation and key assumptions | Impact | Cost | Cost/dog | High Priority | owner, e.g. "3.6 DAS should provide enhanced behavior training ... 700-1300 adoptions/yr; $392k-$770k/yr; $556-$605 [per dog]" ([BCG Dallas deck](https://byu-strategy.github.io/management-consulting/resources/reference-decks/bcg-loose-dogs-dallas.pdf)). It is the only place where impact, cost, timeframe and owner sit together for every recommendation, and in both cases its total reconciles to the headline number in the executive summary (the NDIA appendix repeats the "~$250-420m per annum" paragraph verbatim).

The published record of the other firms shows the same skeleton with house variations. Deloitte's 2025 assurance review for Australia's Department of Employment and Workplace Relations orders its chapters Executive Summary (p. 5), Assurance Outcomes (15), Insights (17), Recommendations (23), and only then Overview of the subject (38), Background & Methodology (44), Analysis (50) and Legislative & Policy Traceability (51), with the formal opinion shipped as a separate Statement of Assurance ([DEWR](https://www.dewr.gov.au/node/17099)). Oliver Wyman's 2022 feasibility report for the California Department of Insurance opens with a dated cover letter and reliance language, presents "five different options for establishing a statewide long-term care insurance program", defers cost to a later actuarial report, and attributes the recommendations to the Task Force rather than the firm ([Oliver Wyman AB 567 Feasibility Report](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/0500-about-us/03-appointments/upload/AB567OliverWymanFeasibilityReport2022.pdf)). McKinsey's 115-page Missouri Medicaid "Rapid Response Review" (February 2019) is stamped "PREDECISIONAL" and runs Executive Summary, Overview, MMIS, Operations, Implementation Considerations ([Missouri DSS](https://dss.mo.gov/mhd/mt/docs/mhd-rapid-response-review.pdf); [MFFH](https://mffh.org/news/keeping-people-at-the-center-medicaid-in-missouri/)). Two further facts about ownership matter for an AI consultancy's product design: the bottom tier is routinely withheld as the firm's property (the Greater London Authority's cover notes for McKinsey's steering-committee sessions state "Appendices are the property of McKinsey & Company and are reserved from publication" ([GLA](https://www.london.gov.uk/moderngovmb/documents/s36287/Item%204%20-%20Test%20emerging%20story%20Session.pdf))), and clients increasingly want the deliverable in their own skin: Canada's immigration department told Parliament that "as work with IRCC progressed and by IRCC direction, McKinsey removed their brand and used IRCC's logo/templates" and "IRCC took ownership over the development of the products" ([IRCC](https://www.ourcommons.ca/content/Committee/441/OGGO/WebDoc/WD12418565/12418565/DepartmentOfCitizenshipAndImmigration-McKinseyDocsWithIRCCLogo-b.pdf)).

## The executive summary is a decision document that reads in one page

The firms' summary is built on Minto's Pyramid Principle, developed at McKinsey between 1963 and 1973: ideas "organized as a pyramid under a single point", with the introduction framed by the "Situation, Complication, Question" sequence ([barbaraminto.com](https://www.barbaraminto.com/)). Her second rule is that "ideas at any level in the pyramid must always be summaries of the ideas grouped below them", which bans what she calls intellectually blank assertions such as "We recommend five changes" in favour of a summary that carries the insight, "The main problems we are facing are all related to profitability" ([StrategyU](https://strategyu.co/pyramid-principle-2/)). Ex-MBB practitioners translate that into a one-slide (occasionally two) Situation–Complication–Resolution page in bold-claim-plus-bullet format: "Write the most important takeaway of each section as bolded text, then add numbers and supporting facts as bullets. Read only the bolded text and check that the story holds together", with the summary sitting before the table of contents, carrying no visuals beyond icons, and functioning as "a decision document" rather than "a play-by-play summary of a project" ([Slideworks](https://slideworks.io/resources/how-to-write-executive-summary)). The Resolution "should account for 60-70% of your executive summary", the Situation "One to two sentences maximum", the whole page "300-500 words max" with "No more than 3-4 key points", and methodology and caveats are banished to the body ([Deckary](https://deckary.com/blog/executive-summary-slides)). Umbrex's test is that the summary must "carry the deck's argument rather than its table of contents", lead "with the decision the executive must approve, understand, challenge, or choose", and cover both "so what" and "now what" ([Umbrex](https://umbrex.com/resources/the-consulting-career-guide/storylining-and-deck-structure/)).

The real documents show the principle operating at section level rather than literally on page one, because a commissioned public report must put the mandate on record first. McKinsey's Lebanon slide 7 is the purest one-page form: three labelled blocks, "Vicious Economic Cycle — The Lebanese economy has been going through a vicious cycle, driven by: a highly volatile economy reliant on diaspora inflows rather than productive sectors...", "Promising Future — To overcome the economic challenges, Lebanon should develop a National Vision guided by: consistent principles; indicators to be monitored...", and "Focused Approach — Five sectors could have a significant contribution ... an effective institutionalization mechanism should be set-up", and those three labels then become the three sections of the 150-slide summary ([Lebanon Economic Vision](https://www.economy.gov.lb/media/11893/20181022-1228full-report-en.pdf)). The NDIA summary is the six-page prose equivalent: capitalised sub-heads run THE NDIS AND PRICING (situation), THE INDEPENDENT PRICING REVIEW PROCESS (mandate, with the Terms of Reference reproduced and the fieldwork counted: "engaged with over 1000 individuals across Australia through 10 open forums, 9 webinars and 45 one-on-one interviews"), FEEDBACK FROM CONSULTATION and KEY FINDINGS (complication), RECOMMENDATIONS framed as "three steps", and IMPLICATIONS closing on the number, "potential financial impact of ~$250-420m per annum over the next 12 to 24 months" with "Approximately 50% of the total cost implication ... is temporary" ([McKinsey IPR Final Report](https://www.ndis.gov.au/media/359/download)). BCG's Dallas deck shows the variant for a briefing where the recommendation has its own section: slide 8 "Executive Findings" carries bold claims with quantified sub-bullets ("~85% of dogs in southern Dallas not spayed or neutered", "Census estimates ~8,700 loose dogs in southern Dallas", "bites from loose-owned dogs have increased 23% annually", "DAS field intake has fallen ~4% annually") and the answer waits until slide 25 ([BCG Dallas deck](https://byu-strategy.github.io/management-consulting/resources/reference-decks/bcg-loose-dogs-dallas.pdf)).

The summary is the last thing written and the first thing agreed. McKinsey teams draft the storyline as a two-level "dot-dash" outline (main ideas as dots, "2nd-level, supporting ideas, facts, and exhibits ... indented and designated with dashes") that "provide the structure around which the PowerPoint deck should be built" ([Working with McKinsey](https://workingwithmckinsey.blogspot.com/2013/07/McKinsey-storyline-dot-dash.html)), then a "ghost deck", "about 20% complete", in which only the leads and headlines are written and pages are "stickered" with what data exists and what is missing, reviewed with the partner to ask "What are we missing, and what do we need to do to get it?" ([Working with McKinsey](https://workingwithmckinsey.blogspot.com/2013/07/McKinsey-presentations-ghost-decks.html)). The London Enterprise Panel record shows the same staging with the client: six steering-committee sessions named Kick-off, Early insights, Share factbase, Test emerging story, Share priorities, and Plan & metrics ([GLA](https://www.london.gov.uk/moderngovmb/documents/s35689/Item%204%20-%20Early%20Insights%20Session%20Incl.%20Objectives%20Project%20Plan.pdf)). An AI consultancy that produces a finished document in one pass skips the step the firms consider the product: a storyline the client has already rejected and repaired twice before the exhibits exist.

## Every page is built so a partner can reject it in thirty seconds

The page-level canon is small and uniform across firms. Minto supplies the logic, Gene Zelazny (McKinsey's long-time director of visual communications) the chart rules ("Move from the message to its comparison type and then select a chart form that expresses that comparison"; "Use a title that conveys the chart's takeaway rather than merely naming the data") ([antoinebuteau.com](https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-gene-zelazny/)), and Ethan Rasiel the discipline of "One Message per Chart" and "Prewire the Message: Share major findings with key stakeholders before the final meeting so objections can be surfaced" ([antoinebuteau.com](https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-ethan-m-rasiel/)). The action title is "a short, simple sentence" stating the takeaway, "under 15 words", never more than two lines, and the instruction when it does not fit is to split the slide, because "The wrong way to handle this is to shrink the title font size" ([Slideworks](https://slideworks.io/resources/how-to-write-action-titles-like-mckinsey)). The standalone test is that "Read only the slide titles. They should form a coherent narrative" and "If the deck has ten slides, you should be able to explain its logic in roughly ten sentences" ([Umbrex](https://umbrex.com/resources/the-consulting-career-guide/storylining-and-deck-structure/)). The exhibit's "only job is to prove the title", and "When in doubt, use a column chart" ([StrategyU](https://strategyu.co/think-before-you-chart)). Page furniture is fixed: action title top left, sticker top right, footnotes then source bottom left, page number bottom right, with stickers there "to describe the nature of the information and to shield yourself from problems in the future" ([Consultport](https://consultport.com/for-consultants/consulting-presentation-checklist-make-sure-to-include-these-7-elements/)).

Real decks show one refinement the guides omit: the action title is often a two-part construction, a navigational prefix keyed to the framework followed by a full-sentence message. The verbatim titles below are from published firm documents.

The reviewer's send-back triggers follow directly: a topic label such as "Market Overview" signals "the deck is still descriptive"; a title that "could apply to almost any page" is too generic; a title with an "and" or a semicolon holds two claims and the slide must split; "No title should promise more than the chart delivers"; and "unsourced numbers destroy credibility" ([Umbrex](https://umbrex.com/resources/the-consulting-career-guide/building-strong-consulting-slides/); [Deckary](https://deckary.com/blog/consulting-quality-slides)). The self-check a manager applies is "Can my manager understand the point within 30 seconds?" and "Could I defend the evidence if the client challenged it?" ([Umbrex](https://umbrex.com/resources/the-consulting-career-guide/building-strong-consulting-slides/)). Recommendation pages are a parallel to-do list of imperative verbs, each tied to earlier data, and the next-steps page answers "What do we need to do on Monday" ([Slideworks](https://slideworks.io/resources/how-to-write-recommendation-slides)); McKinsey's Stop TB next-steps page is a two-column table of action and description, e.g. "Complete performance dialogue workshop — Conduct 2 hour workshop with GDF team leads..." followed by a "Next steps and expected impact" page stickered PRELIMINARY ([Stop TB deck](https://www.stoptb.org/sites/default/files/imported/document/BoardDocs/17/2.09-03_Strengthening_Performance_Management_in_the_Partnership/2.09-03.1_Helping_Global_Health_Partnerships_to_Increase_their_Impact_0.pdf)).

## Client data, benchmarks and assumptions are separated by wording and position, not colour

No firm uses icons or colour to distinguish the client's numbers from external benchmarks from the team's own guesses; the distinction is carried entirely by where text sits and what it says, and the best public evidence is McKinsey's 124-page NHS productivity deck of March 2009, a working draft leaked and then published by the Department of Health, which is candid precisely because it was never meant to be seen ([NHS deck](https://hsj.co.uk/download?ac=1216888)). Three devices do the work. First, the source line names each dataset and year and ends with the firm's computation: "SOURCE: The Information Centre for Health and Social Care 2007 – Workforce Census; National Audit Office – Summarised Accounts Care purchased by PCTs; HES Online; McKinsey analysis" (PDF p. 16), with "McKinsey analysis" appearing on roughly 30 of 124 pages and "Source: Team analysis" or "Source: Wards observation" where the evidence was the team's own ([NHS deck](https://hsj.co.uk/download?ac=1216888)). Second, assumptions sit in numbered footnotes beginning "Assumes": "1 2.5% inflation, except for drugs 5.5%; activity growth based on 98-06 trend. Assumes spend and allocations balanced in 2009/10 and 2010/11 Note: Excludes NHS pensions (£14bn), Capital Expenditure (£4.5bn)" (PDF p. 8) ([NHS deck](https://hsj.co.uk/download?ac=1216888)). Third, and most important for an AI consultancy, the backup "Sources" tables (PDF pp. 109–112) list every metric in four columns, Source | Year | Metric used in calculations | Data used, and **the word "Assumption" appears literally in the source column whenever the number is the team's**: "Assumption based on one PCT Provider Arm | 2007/08 | % of staff costs over total costs | 75%"; "Assumption – reduction of variability in DN productivity; experience in community services | N.a. | % of potential staff productivity improvement | 11–15%"; "Typical practice | N.a. | % of GPs staff costs over total costs | 60%"; alongside hard data rows such as "HES online | 2007/08 | Total number of FCEs by acute provider | 14 million" ([NHS deck](https://hsj.co.uk/download?ac=1216888)).

Benchmarks are drawn first from the client's own population and only then from outside. The NHS deck's productivity benchmark is a percentile chart of the client's own trusts ("264 Top decile, 231 Top quartile, 204 Median, 159 Bottom quartile, 46 Bottom decile — Current productivity levels, FCE/staff member", PDF p. 17); external comparators are labelled by type rather than name ("Hospital X, Hospital Y, Hospital Z, Canadian hospital, U.S. AMC example, High volume example, Theoretical capacity", PDF p. 21); and borrowed experience states its scope, "2–3% potential savings on current PCT commissioning spend (c70b) based on experience in Germany and US where savings of 3–5% have been achieved at the end of a 2-year programme" (PDF p. 113) ([NHS deck](https://hsj.co.uk/download?ac=1216888)). The McKinsey Global Institute's 44-page technical appendix to "Lions on the Move II" is the prose form of the same discipline: it "takes a chapter-by-chapter approach to outlining the methodology, data sources, and assumptions for each of the significant pieces of analysis", narrates its data cleaning ("We gave all data a new baseline of 2015 real dollars"), flags proprietary inputs ("McKinsey's proprietary procurement benchmarks by sector"), states comparability adjustments ("we focused on companies earning more than $1 billion in revenue per year, and excluded multinational corporations and SOEs"), and labels weak assumptions as weak: "Although a crude assumption, this approach was necessary in the absence of more recent household surveys in Africa" and "This methodology can be considered to be fairly accurate for lower income levels, but becomes less accurate for the more than $20,000 category" ([MGI technical appendix](https://karriere.mckinsey.de/~/media/mckinsey/featured%20insights/middle%20east%20and%20africa/lions%20on%20the%20move%20realizing%20the%20potential%20of%20africas%20economies/mgi%20lions%20on%20the%20move%202_technical%20appendix_september%202016.pdf)). The lesson the firms learned the hard way is that "X analysis" must be backed by a table showing which inputs are external and which are assumed; without it, a stack of "McKinsey analysis" lines invites exactly the charge that a client cannot verify the foundation.

The size of the prize is built bottom-up as a visible formula and shown as a range on every bar. The NHS deck's lever page reads "9–14%* opportunity to improve productivity X 50% of budget X £33b acute commissioning spending = £1.5–2.4bn" under the percentile chart, with the asterisk resolved in a footnote that states exactly how the range was made: "Top of range: bottom performers stepping up to 80% of the median (e.g., for doctors from 159 to 195). Bottom of the range: bottom performers step up to 50% of the median" (PDF p. 17) ([NHS deck](https://hsj.co.uk/download?ac=1216888)). The headline page stacks the levers ("Total potential efficiencies 13.4 – 19.9 ... 15-22%" of spend) under an "ESTIMATE" sticker, bands them by timing ("1-3 years / 3-5 years / + 5 years") and captions them "Potential size of the opportunity; part cash, part reinvested in improved care" (PDF p. 10); investment is netted on separate pages ("additional estates costs of £165m would be incurred", PDF p. 44) and dependencies between levers are written down ("Assumes previous opportunities for efficiency and effectiveness improvement are achieved", PDF p. 78) ([NHS deck](https://hsj.co.uk/download?ac=1216888)). The ranges are scenario spreads produced by flexing one capture assumption, not statistical intervals, and MGI narrates its ranges the same way: "We used the IHS forecast of production growth for the lower bound and estimated the upper bound to match GDP growth of about 4 percent" ([MGI technical appendix](https://karriere.mckinsey.de/~/media/mckinsey/featured%20insights/middle%20east%20and%20africa/lions%20on%20the%20move%20realizing%20the%20potential%20of%20africas%20economies/mgi%20lions%20on%20the%20move%202_technical%20appendix_september%202016.pdf)). Tornado charts and formal base/downside/upside cases belong to the business-case genre rather than the sizing deck; the practitioner rules there are "Identify 3–5 key assumptions and run sensitivities, such as varying them over 50–150% of base", "Separate gross from net benefits, model leakage", choose a benefit ramp "that reflects how change actually happens, not the one that flatters the NPV", and "Every headline figure in the executive summary should trace back to the model and an assumptions log" ([Umbrex](https://umbrex.com/resources/the-busy-consultants-guide-to-writing-business-cases/benefits-value-case/)).

Options are laid out before the recommendation, scored on pre-defined independent criteria, and stickered "For discussion" so the client can challenge criteria and weights before the firm commits ([Consultport](https://consultport.com/for-consultants/consulting-presentation-checklist-make-sure-to-include-these-7-elements/)). The deck-native form is a Harvey-ball matrix with a weighted-score row ([Deckary](https://deckary.com/blog/harvey-balls-powerpoint)) or a two-by-two of impact versus ease ([Slideworks](https://slideworks.io/resources/mckinsey-problem-solving-process)). Public-sector commissioners now demand more than that. HM Treasury's 2024 guidance says "Criteria should be mutually independent", that "weights for criteria must not be derived from their relative importance alone" but from swing weighting (the spread between best and worst option on each criterion times how much that spread matters), that results must be sense-checked with stakeholders and then stress-tested by varying the weights, and that "simple scoring and weighting ... is NOT compliant with the latest Green Book advice due to its 'lack of transparency and objectivity'" ([HM Treasury MCDA guidance](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6645e4b2b7249a4c6e9d3631/Use_of_MCDA_in_options_appraisal_of_economic_cases.pdf)). The National Audit Office found the baseline routinely missing: the short-list must contain a "do nothing/minimum" option, yet "Only five of the 45 final Impact Assessments we reviewed monetised either the costs or benefits of the 'do nothing/minimum' option", and sensitivity ranges were often "arbitrary, indicating that the sensitivity analysis was seen as little more than a tick-box exercise" ([NAO](https://www.nao.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2011/05/1012_Option_Appraisal.pdf)). A deliverable that shows the do-nothing case, the swing-weight rationale and a weight-sensitivity test already exceeds what most incumbent deliverables provide.

## Uncertainty is marked in four layers, never as a confidence score

The firms do not attach a confidence rating to a number. They mark uncertainty structurally in four layers: a page-level sticker, a word in the title or footnote, notation on the number itself, and a methodology note stating assumptions. The vocabulary of stickers is defined by practitioners and attested in real decks. "Preliminary" means the information "has not come to final conclusions"; "Indicative" means the numbers "may not be completely accurate, but they are accurate enough to point us in the right direction"; "Illustrative" means they are "hypothetical", possibly "completely made up" to show a relationship; "For discussion" flags content that is controversial and needs input ([Consultport](https://consultport.com/for-consultants/consulting-presentation-checklist-make-sure-to-include-these-7-elements/)). In McKinsey's 2020 FCC deck the framework pages carry "Preliminary, proprietary, pre-decisional" together with "Non-exhaustive", time-sensitive pages carry "Current as of August 5, 2020", and the cover "CONFIDENTIAL" ([McKinsey FCC deck](https://www.federalcitycouncil.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/McKinsey_Transportation.pdf)). The 2009 Stop TB deck stamps "PRELIMINARY" on unfinished sections, "DUMMY NUMBERS" on dashboard mock-ups and "(backup)" in supporting-page titles, with "XX" placeholders in KPI tables ([Stop TB deck](https://www.stoptb.org/sites/default/files/imported/document/BoardDocs/17/2.09-03_Strengthening_Performance_Management_in_the_Partnership/2.09-03.1_Helping_Global_Health_Partnerships_to_Increase_their_Impact_0.pdf)). The DECC report uses "ILLUSTRATIVE" on worked examples and repeats "1 Estimated on 2030 'policy off' baseline" under every abatement exhibit ([UK electricity efficiency report](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a78a166e5274a277e68e3be/7035-capturing-full-elec-eff-potential-edr.pdf)). The NHS deck stamps "ESTIMATE" on every sizing page, "ILLUSTRATIVE" on the external-support matrix, prints "Working Draft - Last Modified 18/03/2009 15:41:52" in the footer of 122 of 124 pages, and leaves unresolved items visibly open with "(TBC)" and "(???)"; the words "preliminary", "indicative", "confidence", "directional", "validated" and "caveat" appear nowhere in it ([NHS deck](https://hsj.co.uk/download?ac=1216888)).

Precision itself is a signal: real pages deliberately mix exact sourced figures ("248,311" jobs) with rounded modelled ones ("~$250 million"), so the number format tells the reader which is which ([McKinsey FCC deck](https://www.federalcitycouncil.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/McKinsey_Transportation.pdf)). The written rule behind it is to avoid "false precision", which occurs "when estimates appear more accurate than the data supports", and to triangulate: "When different sources point to similar conclusions, confidence increases even without precision" ([CaseBasix](https://www.casebasix.com/pages/consultants-incomplete-data)). Umbrex asks writers to calibrate with phrases such as "Initial analysis suggests..." and to ask in review "whether certainty is overstated" ([Umbrex](https://umbrex.com/resources/the-consulting-career-guide/storylining-and-deck-structure/)). MGI's automation work shows the house way of presenting a forecast spread: a midpoint inside an explicitly named early-to-late adoption range rather than an interval, with the range explained by its drivers ("the rate at which generative AI is adopted, investment decisions and regulation") ([Chui testimony](https://lhc.ca.gov/wp-content/uploads/sites/lhc.ca.gov/files/Reports/245/WrittenTestimony/ChuiJan2018.pdf)). McKinsey's own strategy practice concedes that even this is rarer than it should be: in the strategy room uncertainty is "rarely accounted for in a clear and robust way" and "the most likely version of the future" is presented as the only view "with nothing about scenarios or ranges" ([McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/how-to-confront-uncertainty-in-your-strategy)). Two things the firms conspicuously do not do are worth stating for product design: no consulting deliverable or guide located applies red-amber-green ratings to data quality or confidence (the only RAG examples are public-sector data-assurance guides such as [LPFT](https://www.lpft.nhs.uk/index.php?cID=2134)), and "directional" and "to be validated" are spoken vocabulary rather than page labels; the attested equivalents are "Indicative", "ESTIMATE" and "(TBC)". A per-input data-quality column would therefore be an addition to the convention, and a defensible one.

## The handover is a management system, not a Gantt chart

What the firms hand over at the end of the strategy phase is the design of an implementation system, with the detailed workplan explicitly assigned to the client. BCG's Dallas deck gives the prioritisation (quick wins such as "3.1 Digital marketing", medium-term such as "2.1 Add more ASOs", strategic priorities such as "6.1 DAS as independent department"), the funding ("$20.9MM" split across city, DAS and spay/neuter with "Surge spending on S/N not necessary after 3 years"), the asks by stakeholder, and a next-steps page organised as "Assign ownership" ("engage in a full-day summit to align on: specific individual owners for each initiative"), "Start small" ("establish the minimal viable version of a recommendation") and "Track Progress" ("regular and frequent progress reporting for each initiative; reprioritize resources based on realized value and potential") ([BCG Dallas deck](https://byu-strategy.github.io/management-consulting/resources/reference-decks/bcg-loose-dogs-dallas.pdf)); the City Manager then produced the implementation plan in September 2016, updates in January and April 2017 and a dashboard in June 2018 ([Slideworks](https://slideworks.io/resources/what-real-consulting-deliverables-look-like-bcg-example)). McKinsey's Lebanon deck hands over a governance design (Council of Ministers → "Performance Management and Delivery Unit (PMDU)" with a Secretary General "mandated with fixed renewable terms" → sector task forces → ministries, marked "Initial and still under discussion"), an interim versus end-state option, a dated table of "9 key activities" to be completed by end-2019 ("Recruit & train the interim economic team – 31 Dec 2018"; "Issue quarterly reports on the progress of the Vision – 30 Jun 2019"), and tasks the client's task forces with "developing 3-foot plans"; a weekly Gantt with milestone flags ("Aug 1: Project Kick-off", "Sep 1: Zone plan & location finalized") appears only for the flagship projects (slides 143–149) ([Lebanon Economic Vision](https://www.economy.gov.lb/media/11893/20181022-1228full-report-en.pdf)).

When the firm stays for implementation, the unit of work and its paperwork are standardised. BCG's nomenclature runs "program, workstream, initiative, project, and milestone", a workstream being a "Broad ownership silo ... connected to P&L and/or budget ownership" and an initiative a "Specific area of opportunity" such as "Cut cost of office supplies by 20%"; "Each project ... has a charter developed at the initial idea-generation stage, as well as a business case and milestones", and within three to four months the portfolio sits "loaded into a transformation management software program" ([BCG 2021](https://web-assets.bcg.com/90/88/be71e75d4299a65760a5e41c12fe/how-the-best-set-up-their-program-journey-nov-2021.pdf)). McKinsey's stage gates run L0 Idea, L1 Identified ("preliminary sizing"), L2 Planned ("Approval of business case" by sponsor, finance, HR), L3 Executed, L4 Realized, L5 Validated ("Value realized in actual cash flows"), with "an average of four milestones" per initiative and the owner providing "a monthly schedule of expected value to be captured on the bottom line" ([McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/rts/our-insights/keeping-transformations-on-target)). The reason the register must carry ranges and the pipeline must be oversized is written in the same source: **"L1 estimations usually fall by about 70 percent by the time they reach L5. Organizations will therefore need a pipeline with a total value that's more than three times the initial target"**, "only 29 percent of the metrics organizations claim to follow are actively used", and only "30 percent of transformations deliver their intended benefits" ([McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/rts/our-insights/keeping-transformations-on-target)). Baselines belong to finance: last year's reported financials are "preferable to using forecasts or budgets", unrelated events must be stripped out, and gains must be "translated into formal budget commitments" or they leak back when "the plant budget was unchanged" ([McKinsey](https://karriere.mckinsey.de/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/why-cfos-need-a-bigger-role-in-business-transformations)). BCG's best practice is "not to allow initiatives to proceed without the finance department's explicit approval of the business case", and its data say that applying "qualitatively different impact metrics ... to the same initiative" makes it "40% less likely to succeed" ([BCG 2021](https://web-assets.bcg.com/90/88/be71e75d4299a65760a5e41c12fe/how-the-best-set-up-their-program-journey-nov-2021.pdf)).

The governance cadence is deliberately intense and produces a standard status report. McKinsey's transformation office runs "standardized, weekly, action-oriented meetings" of 60 to 90 minutes, expects "80 percent of the initiatives across a program to be updated with specific actions every week", and holds "a single source of truth" ([McKinsey](https://karriere.mckinsey.de/capabilities/transformation/our-insights/the-role-of-the-transformation-office)). BCG schedules a "Weekly steering committee (2 hours)" attended by the CEO or CFO sponsor "to make the decisions that no one else can", insists the centrepiece of meetings is "the centralized data system ... not newly created PowerPoint decks", and publishes the only firm-authored status layout found: **Initiative | Project | Owner | Plan G4 date | Forecast G4 date | Plan 20XX EBITDA | FC 20XX EBITDA | Status in tool (On track / Off track / Completed) | Comments**, with a companion "Milestones Due" report (Project name | Project owner | Milestone owner | Milestone | Plan Date | Forecast Date) ([BCG 2021](https://web-assets.bcg.com/90/88/be71e75d4299a65760a5e41c12fe/how-the-best-set-up-their-program-journey-nov-2021.pdf)). Honesty rules are explicit: "Yellow is the new green", "watermelons" (green outside, red inside) are the enemy, and "Even in a well-run program, 20% of initiatives won't achieve their desired impact, and even more initiatives (30%) will typically be at risk"; risks are surfaced by a sub-one-hour "rigor test" and DICE scores, and escalated by "Boomeranging" so staff "can circle back to those with decision-making authority" ([BCG 2021](https://web-assets.bcg.com/90/88/be71e75d4299a65760a5e41c12fe/how-the-best-set-up-their-program-journey-nov-2021.pdf)). Deloitte's 156-page Minnesota implementation plan shows the formal written version: six concurrent threads across three phases on a 36-month horizon, a project charter with twelve named components including a "Risk Strategy Statement" and "Critical Success Factors" (p. 59), a plan with WBS, baseline schedule, risk, issue, quality and change-control plans with a signed change-request form (pp. 60–66), a roles table distinguishing Sponsor, Steering Committee and Project Manager (p. 57), and a roll-out handbook "with a site readiness checklist, a day-by-day schedule" updated with pilot lessons learned (pp. 72–77) ([Deloitte Minnesota plan](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2024/Other/240938/vol3.pdf)). No firm publication names a "decision log"; decisions are recorded through gate approvals, SteerCo minutes and signed change requests.

On the exit itself the published guidance is thin, and BCG is the most explicit: around the one-year mark "this is often the time when the organization retakes control of the TO", which holds only "With good knowledge transfer and training (including job shadowing, in many cases)", "detailed documentation about transformation processes" and "a well-thought-out set of role charters and job specifications" ([BCG 2021](https://web-assets.bcg.com/90/88/be71e75d4299a65760a5e41c12fe/how-the-best-set-up-their-program-journey-nov-2021.pdf)). McKinsey's capability transfer is "field-and-forum", formal classroom training plus "informal on-the-job training through working in a combined team with our consultants" ([McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/careers/life-at-mckinsey/our-culture-and-communities/careers-blog/aleck)); Bain's Results Delivery, per a secondary summary, ends by shrinking the office, handing "ownership to the line" and keeping "a light quarterly review" ([Umbrex](https://umbrex.com/resources/frameworks/project-management-frameworks/bain-results-delivery/)). Pilots are judged at a gate against the finance-verified target and the milestones in the pilot plan ("the location, length, and goals of any pilot") rather than against a published scorecard, and Bain argues small pilots mislead, preferring a "hothouse" of "average processes, average customers and average locations across a large enough sample to represent the whole network" ([BCG 2021](https://web-assets.bcg.com/90/88/be71e75d4299a65760a5e41c12fe/how-the-best-set-up-their-program-journey-nov-2021.pdf); [Bain](https://www.bain.com/el/insights/banks-should-scrap-pilot-programs-and-do-this-instead-american-banker/)). The assembled handover package is therefore: the recommendation register with ranges, owners and timeframes; a governance chart with decision rights; a finance-owned baseline and budget commitments; initiative charters loaded through the first gates; the status-report format and cadence stepping down from weekly to quarterly; role charters; playbooks; and a training plan. No firm publishes an exit checklist or acceptance criteria for handover, which leaves room for an AI consultancy to make one explicit.

## Clients pay for challenge and accountability, and now check the footnotes first

The documented critique of incumbent deliverables has three strands. The long-form one is Mazzucato and Collington's: "often this kind of advice and consulting is literally just a cut-and-paste PowerPoint", fees that "are actually almost rents in the system", and a relationship that is "hollowing out capacity internally" because "the less you do, the less you learn" ([The New Republic](https://newrepublic.com/article/171110/consultants-mangle-government-big-con-mazzucato-collingworth-interview)). The survey strand shows a persistent value gap: across roughly 10,000 senior buyers a year, **about 50% say the firm created value above fees, 42% say value matched fees and 9% say they overpaid; for US management-consulting firms only 35% say value exceeded fees and 23% say fees exceeded value**, while the Nordics (76%) and Germany (67%), where procurement forces an explicit value conversation, score far higher ([Source Global Research](https://www.sourceglobalresearch.com/?p=138877)). A JAMA study published in May 2026 of 306 US nonprofit hospitals that hired a management consultant for the first time, against 513 matched controls, found "no evidence of meaningful changes" in finances, operations or quality after more than $7.8bn of spend ([UChicago Medicine](https://uchicagomedicine.org/forefront/research-and-discoveries-articles/nonprofit-hospitals-spend-billions-on-management-consultants)). What clients say they value is the mirror image: a client summarising the engagement in "three simple yet truly profound words: They Challenged us!" ([CMC-Global](https://www.cmc-global.org/content/consulting-value-eyes-beholders)); "consultants who have the guts to talk openly about how the project is going" ([emagine](https://int-ssl.emagine.org/newsroom/article/16/46/what-clients-want)); objectivity, which 84% want and 47% believe they get ([management.co.nz](https://management.co.nz/?p=3699)); and, in a ranked Australian survey of 171 top-500 executives, listening and understanding first, with technical knowledge only fifth ([UTS](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/handle/10453/18864)). Boards and lenders still want decks that "work as standalone documents" with explicit assumptions, even as tech executives read the same features as defensive ([Reddit snapshot](https://reddit.sentinel-team.org/posts/1t1kw8z/snapshots/2026-05-05T02%3A12%3A10.954715Z)).

The third strand is new, concrete and checkable in minutes. In October 2025 Deloitte Australia's AU$440,000, 273-page assurance review for DEWR was found to contain more than a dozen bogus references, citations to imaginary reports by named professors, a fictional extract from a court case and a fabricated quote attributed to nonexistent paragraphs of a judgment; the corrected version disclosed, on page 58 of 273, "a generative AI large language model (Azure OpenAI GPT-4o) based tool chain" used to fill "traceability and documentation gaps", and Deloitte refunded the final instalment while the department insisted "there are no changes to the recommendations" ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2025/10/06/deloitte_ai_report_australia/)). In November 2025 Deloitte Canada's C$1.6m health-workforce plan for Newfoundland and Labrador cited research articles that do not exist, some attributed to real researchers who never wrote them; Deloitte said AI "was not used to write the report" but was "selectively used to support a small number of research citations" ([The Independent (NL)](https://theindependent.ca/news/lji/deloitte-breaks-silence-on-n-l-healthcare-report/)), and the province overhauled its procurement process in March 2026 ([CP24](https://www.cp24.com/news/canada/2026/03/11/newfoundland-and-labrador-overhauls-procurement-after-report-errors-thought-to-be-ai/)). In July 2026 four PwC Middle East reports were found to carry fake footnotes and unverifiable sources, one footnote URL containing "utm_source=chatgpt.com" ([Irish Times](https://www.irishtimes.com/business/2026/07/29/pwc-published-thought-leadership-reports-marred-by-ai-hallucinations/)). The buyer's reaction is measurable: in a Source survey of 3,887 companies **70% said they would not trust a report produced with AI**, almost a third said learning a firm had used AI in a report would reduce their confidence in it, and clients who had used consultants' AI tools were more sceptical, not less ([Resultsense](https://www.resultsense.com/news/2026-08-04-consultancy-clients-distrust-ai-reports/)). UK and US clients split almost evenly on whether AI-produced solutions will be better (49%) or "more generic" (51%), and the half expecting better are the ones willing to pay more; "Expertise and accountability remain crucial to clients" ([Source Global Research](https://sourceglobalresearch.com/market-update/will-the-efficiency-gains-from-ai-destroy-the-professional-services-industry-probably-not)).

The incumbents have meanwhile automated the parts critics called deckware. McKinsey's Lilli is used monthly by over 75% of staff, generates PowerPoint slides from short prompts, runs a "Tone of Voice" check and is the only tool permitted to hold confidential client data; Kate Smaje's line is "Do we need armies of business analysts creating PowerPoints? No, the technology could do that" ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2025/06/02/mckinsey-ai-consulting-powerpoints-proposal-technology)). Bob Sternfels describes the firm as "40,000 humans and 20,000 agents" and says it is "migrating pretty quickly away from ... pure advisory work" toward engagements where McKinsey "will underwrite the outcomes of that business case" ([OfficeChai](https://officechai.com/ai/mckinsey-now-has-60000-people-but-20000-of-them-are-ai-agents-mckinseys-bob-sternfels)). BCG runs Deckster and GENE and an OpenAI collaboration, Bain runs Sage on OpenAI, Strategy& uses Copilot ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2025/06/02/mckinsey-ai-consulting-powerpoints-proposal-technology); [BCG](https://bcg.com/capabilities/artificial-intelligence/openai-collaboration)), and Accenture reported generative-AI bookings of $5.9bn in FY2025 ([Accenture](https://investor.accenture.com/~/media/Files/A/accenture-v4/investors/home/accenture-2025-letter-to-shareholders.pdf)). Buyers expect the price to follow: 89% expect consultancies to use AI and 73% want new pricing models ([Consultancy.uk](https://www.consultancy.uk/news/38655/clients-increasingly-expect-consultants-to-be-ai-powered-but-also-believe-pricing-must-adapt)).

The controlled evidence on what AI alone cannot do is the BCG/Harvard field experiment on 758 BCG consultants with GPT-4 in 2023. On tasks inside the model's competence it raised completion by 12.2%, speed by 25.1% and rated quality by 40%, with the weakest consultants gaining most; but on a business case "based on spreadsheet data and interview transcripts requiring triangulation", **consultants without AI were correct 84% of the time and those with AI only 60–70%, "though write-up quality was still higher"**, and the group given prompt training did worse still, read as over-reliance ([One Useful Thing](https://www.oneusefulthing.org/p/centaurs-and-cyborgs-on-the-jagged)). AI output was also "higher quality but somewhat uniform in aggregate", a homogenisation effect that a 19-study meta-analysis finds small, significant and robust ([Tilburg University](https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/generative-ai-makes-creative-output-more-homogeneous/)). An ex-McKinsey reviewer of an open-source "mckinsey-consultant" agent skill put the gap in the firms' own terms: it produces "boilerplate analysis" without "Socratic questioning and thinking", because the value lies in "the questions consultants ask" ([Business Insider via AOL](https://www.aol.com/articles/rise-ai-knock-off-mckinsey-084401333.html)). Every AI-era failure that clients and journalists caught was a checkable fact (a citation, a quote, a paragraph number), found by looking the source up rather than by detecting style ([eWeek](https://eweek.com/news/gptzero-flags-suspected-ai-content-and-false-citations-in-pwc-reports/)).

## Conclusion

The incumbents' deliverable is best understood as a chain of rejectable artefacts: a storyline the client sees before any analysis, a ghost deck of titles before any exhibit, an exhibit that must prove exactly one title, a source line and footnote that let a sceptic re-derive the number, a sticker that says how finished the page is, and a register that reconciles every recommendation to the headline. The bar for an AI-run consultancy is therefore not visual polish, which Lilli and Deckster have already commoditised and which the BCG experiment shows rises even as correctness falls. It is the thing ChatGPT alone cannot supply and the thing clients now test first: a number built from the client's own data, with the benchmark population named, every assumption flagged as such in the source column with its direction of conservatism, the range's flex rule in a footnote, the do-nothing case monetised, and every citation resolvable. Several conventions the firms observe only informally can be made explicit and auditable without breaking the genre: a per-input provenance and quality column where the NHS deck wrote "based on one PCT", an exit checklist where BCG offers only "job shadowing", a weight-sensitivity test where consulting decks stop at Harvey balls, and a declared AI method on the method page rather than on page 58 of 273.

Two implications follow for positioning. First, since half of clients expect AI output to be generic and 70% say they would not trust an AI report, transparency about method combined with visibly client-specific grounding beats concealment; the Deloitte disclosure buried in an appendix is the cautionary case, and McKinsey's move to underwriting outcomes shows where accountability is heading. Second, the staged client interaction (early insights, fact base, emerging story, priorities, plan and metrics) is part of the product, not overhead; an engine that asks the Socratic questions, shows its day-one answer as a range, and lets the client reject the storyline before the deck exists reproduces what the firms' clients say they actually paid for, "They challenged us", and is the one feature a general chatbot cannot be seen to have done.
